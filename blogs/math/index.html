Background: Turning Student Feedback into Better Learning Experiences

A Data Science Ethics Discussion

Key Ethics Concepts in Focus

The UniTre scenario highlights several important data science ethics concepts: privacy, confidentiality, anonymity, re-identification, informed consent, compulsion, transparency, data misuse, bias, fairness, data protection, data governance, accountability, and responsible data analysis.

Student feedback can help a college identify courses that may need updating or redesigning. However, the value of a course improvement survey depends not only on what the institution hopes to achieve, but also on how information is collected, protected, analyzed, and used. The UniTre Course Improvement Survey provides an opportunity to consider how ethical principles can guide data collection and decision-making.

Ethical Issues in the Survey Design

The intended purpose of the survey appears reasonable: executive management wants to understand students’ experiences and determine which courses may need improvement. Nevertheless, a positive purpose does not automatically make every part of the survey ethically appropriate.

** Identity traceability and privacy

Students are required to identify the name of their course and log in with their username and password. These requirements create a concern that students’ identities could be traced, even if the survey is described as anonymous. Course identification may be useful for comparing feedback across courses, but the institution should consider whether it can achieve that goal without collecting or exposing unnecessary identifying information. Intended use is not the same as ethical compliance.

Other Ethical Issues That Could Be Present

**** Re-identification

Even if names are not displayed in the survey results, students in a course with a very small headcount may be identifiable from the course information or the details in a written comment. This is known as re-identification: connecting information that appears anonymous to a particular person. The possibility of being identified could discourage honest feedback and reduce trust in the survey.

**** Compulsion and meaningful choice

The survey is available for a short period. Although the information provided does not explicitly say that students must complete it, the limited time window could make some students feel pressured to respond before they are ready. This may create a sense of subtle compulsion and may not give students a reasonable opportunity to participate at a convenient time. Clear communication about whether participation is voluntary, along with a reasonable completion window, would help address this concern.

**** Data misuse and purpose limitation

Students may expect their feedback to be used to improve courses. However, management could consider using the collected information for other purposes, such as promoting school programs to prospective students. This raises a concern about whether the data is being used in ways students would reasonably expect. The institution should clearly explain the intended uses and carefully consider any proposed secondary use.

**** Bias and the quality of survey questions

Ambiguous or leading questions can influence how students interpret and answer the survey. A question that suggests a preferred answer may discourage students from expressing their genuine experience. Different students may also interpret unclear wording differently, leading to misleading results. Neutral, understandable questions are important if management wants feedback it can rely on.

Recommendations for Executive Management

To make the survey more trustworthy and useful, UniTre should review its structure and design using the following principles:

• Transparency: Explain why the survey is being conducted, what information is collected, who can see the results, and how the data may be used. Students need enough information to make an informed decision about providing feedback.

• Alignment with ethical principles: Review whether the survey’s intended use conflicts with privacy, fairness, or other ethical responsibilities. Consider who could benefit, who could be harmed, and whether decisions based on the results would be fair.

• Clear and unbiased questions: Avoid ambiguous wording that could produce misleading responses. Avoid leading questions that influence student’s responses instead of allowing them to express honest views.

• Data protection: Protect information throughout collection, analysis, storage, and use. Encryption of confidential and identifying information is one possible safeguard; access should also be appropriately limited.

• Privacy and re-identification safeguards: Assess whether course names, small class sizes, login arrangements, or written comments could reveal student identities. Consider removing unnecessary identifying details and combining results for very small classes, introduce bias or noise.

• Data governance: Define who may access raw responses, how the information will be stored, how long it will be retained, and which uses are permitted. Establish procedures for reporting privacy concerns and handling complaints.

If Executive Management Rejects the Recommendations

If management insists that the survey and system have no ethical issues, the analyst should still act professionally and avoid ignoring reasonable concerns. Ethical responsibility does not disappear simply because a concern is rejected by executives.

• Privacy Protection: Protect privacy where possible by limiting access to raw responses, remove unnecessary identifying details, and combine results for small classes where appropriate. Review whether course identification could make students identifiable.

• Apply data governance rules: Document and follow clear rules for access, storage, retention, permitted use, privacy breaches, and complaints.

• Improve survey questions: Review the questions for ambiguity or wording that could pressure students or steer them toward a particular answer. Recommend revisions that support honest and accurate responses.

• Data Analysis Transparency: Be transparent about data analysis, document the methods, assumptions, limitations, and possible biases. Present the findings as evidence that can inform management decisions, not as unquestionable proof that a course or instructor has failed.

• Record and escalate concerns appropriately: Keep a factual record of the risks identified and recommendations made. If serious concerns remain, consider raising them with an appropriate privacy, ethics, or compliance contact, in line with organizational procedures.

Conclusion: Feedback Should Build Trust

A course improvement survey can help UniTre understand students’ experiences and identify opportunities to improve learning. But the institution must consider more than the usefulness of the final results. Students need to understand why their feedback is collected, feel safe enough to answer honestly, and have confidence that their information will be handled responsibly. A survey described as anonymous should be reviewed carefully if login credentials, course details, or small class sizes could make responses traceable.

For the data analyst, the responsibility is to provide useful insights without hiding uncertainty or overstating what the data proves. Transparent communication, fair questions, privacy safeguards, and clear governance can help management make better-informed decisions. Ultimately, ethical data science is not only about collecting information to improve courses; it is also about protecting the people who provide that information. When student trust is respected, feedback is more likely to support fairer decisions and stronger learning experiences.
